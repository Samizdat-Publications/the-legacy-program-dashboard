## National Security Agency

National Security Agency is a institution on the board; board tier B, documented channel and capability, programme role indicated but not yet nailed to a primary source; dates 1952-11-04; axes C1 A3 D1; 11 connections; in the disclosure band.

In one line: Signals intelligence and cybersecurity agency; established 4 November 1952 by classified presidential memorandum.

### What it is

The National Security Agency, established on 4 November 1952 by a classified memorandum from President Truman, and like the National Reconnaissance Office an agency whose name the government preferred for decades not to confirm. Its place in this record is defined by one piece of litigation, Citizens Against UFO Secrecy v. National Security Agency, Civil Action No. 80-1562 in the District Court for the District of Columbia, and by two later events: its appearance among the contributing elements of ODNI's Preliminary Assessment of 25 June 2021, and its absence from the first statutory transfer of UAP records to the National Archives on 24 April 2025.

### Why it is implicated

The operative fact is not that NSA won the case; it is what NSA conceded in order to win it. The Agency located a body of responsive material, withheld it in its entirety, and supported the withholding with a classified in camera affidavit executed by Eugene F. Yeates, Chief of the Office of Policy. The district court reviewed the full text ex parte, the D.C. Circuit affirmed in 1981 and the Supreme Court denied certiorari in 1982. What survives on the public record is therefore an adjudicated position that the Agency held UFO-related documents, that they were classified, and that their number and nature could not be described in open session. Forty years later ODNI named NSA among the elements contributing to the 2021 assessment, which places continuing signals-intelligence equity in the subject. Four years after that, NSA transferred nothing to the National Archives collection built for it.

### The longer account

The three dated events read as one posture held across four decades, and each is a document rather than an allegation.

In 1980 the Agency answered a FOIA suit by conceding that responsive material existed and refusing to characterise it. The Yeates affidavit is the instrument by which a United States intelligence agency established in court that it held UFO material it could not describe publicly, and the public version of that affidavit was released only in heavily redacted form. On this board the resulting edge is graded a directed denial with NSA named as its issuer, because a refusal to describe is a dated, signed, attributable act by the refuser.

In 2021 ODNI's Preliminary Assessment listed NSA among the contributing elements, which is the government saying in its own publication that NSA collection produces UAP-relevant reporting. In 2025 the National Archives announced the first transfer under the statutory UAP records scheme and named only ODNI, the Office of the Secretary of Defense, the FAA and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. An agency with documented equity in the subject produced nothing, and the board carries that null return as the second directed denial on this node.

The Agency's own declassified collection contains one document worth naming for a different reason. It assesses the June 1978 GEPAN report and says that GEPAN's methods make 'the bulk of the Condon Report seem very poor by comparison'. That is a signals agency, in an internal assessment, grading the United States government's own public explanation as the weaker piece of work.

### What it has denied

Two directed denials are recorded on this node and both are refusals to describe rather than assertions that nothing exists. In court the Agency said it had located responsive material and would not characterise it, and won on a showing no member of the public has seen. In 2025 it returned nothing to a statutory records collection built for exactly this subject, while four other bodies returned something. Neither event is NSA saying UAP are not real; both are NSA saying the question of what it holds is not answerable in the open. That is the more informative posture of the two, because it is falsifiable in principle and has never been falsified in practice.

Roles the board assigns it: concealment, disclosure.

- The number and nature of the documents NSA located in the CAUS litigation have never been disclosed. The public version of the Yeates affidavit is heavily redacted and the full text was seen only by the court.
- Whether NSA was asked for records under the statutory UAP collection and declined, or was never tasked, is not established. The National Archives release names who transferred, not who was approached.
- The GEPAN assessment is carried here from a Wayback capture of nsa.gov. Web.archive.org is unreachable from the build environment, so that capture cannot be re-opened from here to confirm the wording independently.

### Why it is implicated

This board argues that cover stories are constructed deliberately at institutional level. ### The longer account

The value of this document to this board is that it removes a step from the argument. The board does not have to establish that the United States government is willing to plan a deception and attribute it to somebody else, because the Joint Chiefs wrote one down and the Chairman signed it. The annex says in its own words that 'Since it would seem desirable to use legitimate provocation as the basis for US military intervention in Cuba a cover and deception plan ... could be executed as an initial effort to provoke Cuban reactions.' Its courses of action include blowing up a US ship in Guantanamo Bay and blaming Cuba, and painting and numbering an aircraft at Eglin AFB as an exact duplicate of a civil registered aircraft belonging to a CIA proprietary organisation in the Miami area. The memorandum also asks for the mission: paragraph 3 recommends that responsibility for both overt and covert military operations be assigned to the Joint Chiefs of Staff. What is on this board is the document and nothing beyond it. Northwoods says nothing about unidentified aerial phenomena and is not offered as evidence about any other case; it is offered as the proven floor of what the institution will put on paper. ## YANKEE BLUE (alleged AFOSI indoctrination program)

YANKEE BLUE (alleged AFOSI indoctrination program) is a program on the board; board tier B, documented channel and capability, programme role indicated but not yet nailed to a primary source; dates 1980 to 2023; axes C1 A3 D3; 11 connections; in the disclosure band.

In one line: Alleged USAF/AFOSI program.

### What it is

YANKEE BLUE is an alleged United States Air Force practice in which personnel entering certain classified units were shown photographs of flying saucers and told they were being read on to a programme to reverse-engineer non-human craft, when no such programme existed. It was reported in June 2025 by The Wall Street Journal as a finding of the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office under its first director, Sean Kirkpatrick, and was said to have been halted by a spring 2023 directive from the office of the Secretary of Defense. No primary document establishing the programme, the practice or the directive has been released.

### Why it is implicated

If documented, YANKEE BLUE would be the single strongest item in the concealment strand of this board, because it is the government manufacturing belief in a recovery and reverse-engineering programme inside its own ranks - which means that some fraction of insider testimony about that programme could be the product of a deliberate indoctrination rather than of access. It cuts both ways and it cuts hard, which is exactly why the tiering here has to be strict. What is documented is not the programme. What is documented is the Department's behaviour when asked for the paper: an Office of the Secretary of Defense FOIA search confined to a single correspondence office returning no records in September 2025, a Pentagon spokesperson on 1 October 2025 stating she could not confirm the memorandum with the word "alleged" underlined for emphasis, and an OSD appellate authority on 12 December 2025 overturning that search as inadequate and remanding for a new one. A department that cannot locate a Secretary-level directive from 2023 has either a records problem or a candour problem, and either is on the map.

### The longer account

This entity is retained on the board with the allegation graded low and the negative findings graded high, and the distinction should be kept in front of the reader at all times. A negative primary finding is a documented fact. It is not a record of the thing.

The allegation entered the public record on 6 June 2025, when Joel Schectman and Aruna Viswanatha of The Wall Street Journal published "The Pentagon Disinformation That Fueled America's UFO Mythology." The reporting described a decades-long pattern in which the Air Force encouraged UFO belief to screen classified aviation programmes, and within that pattern a specific practice: personnel in certain classified units were briefed that they were being admitted to a programme called YANKEE BLUE concerned with reverse-engineering recovered non-human craft, shown photographs of saucers, bound by non-disclosure agreements, and in some accounts warned of extreme consequences for disclosure. The practice was said to have run from roughly the Cold War until 2023, when the office of the Secretary of Defense circulated a directive across the services ordering it to stop immediately. The finding was attributed to AARO's internal historical review under Sean Kirkpatrick.

Everything downstream of that article is either restatement or an attempt to find the paper. Popular Mechanics, VICE and the New York Post ran versions in June 2025; none produced independent documentation. The article itself, as researcher John Greenewald has noted, did not publish the memorandum, did not identify its date and did not describe its sourcing.

The documentary trail that does exist is a FOIA trail, and it is unusually clean because it has been published in full. On 17 June 2025 Greenewald's Black Vault filed a request with the Office of the Secretary of Defense for any directive issued in spring 2023 ordering a halt to YANKEE BLUE-related practices or to other activities involving fabricated claims of reverse-engineering non-human technology. OSD searched solely within its Correspondence Management Division and reported no responsive records. On 17 September 2025 an administrative appeal was filed arguing that a search confined to one office was not "reasonably calculated to uncover all relevant documents" under Oglesby v. U.S. Dep't of Army, 920 F.2d 57 (D.C. Cir. 1990), that a Secretary-level directive could reasonably reside in policy directorates or the OSD Executive Secretariat, and that agencies must pursue logical leads under Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998). The appeal also made the records-management point directly: any such 2023 memorandum would be subject to mandatory federal retention, so its absence is itself a finding.

On 1 October 2025, asked again about the WSJ claim, Pentagon spokesperson Susan Gough replied: "Regarding the alleged memo: I cannot confirm the existence of any department-level memo as described in the article. You may want to ask the Air Force or other military services whether they put out such a memo to their personnel." In the original email the word "alleged" was underlined. This is a carefully constructed sentence. It does not deny that the practice occurred. It denies confirmation of a department-level memorandum and redirects to the services. The Air Force did not respond to a request for comment.

On 12 December 2025 the Acting Chief of the OSD FOIA Division ruled on the appeal: "After carefully considering your appeal, and as a result of discussions between FOID personnel and this office, I am remanding your request to FOID for a search for responsive records." The appellate decision, released as case 25-A-3514-A1, does not establish that the memorandum exists. It establishes that the Department's own appellate authority found the Department's prior categorical no-records answer to rest on an inadequate search, and ordered it done again. As of the last published status the case remains open.

There is a second correction this board has already had to make about itself, and it belongs in the dossier. It has been widely repeated - including previously on this board - that AARO acknowledged YANKEE BLUE in its Report on the Historical Record of U.S. Government Involvement with UAP, Volume I, published 8 March 2024. It did not. A full-text check of the released 63-page volume returns no occurrence of the term, and it appears in none of the FY2023, FY2024 or FY2025 consolidated annual reports. The board logged that correction on 12 August 2026. The only official gesture toward the subject is the Department's statement, made in the context of the June 2025 reporting, that it "is committed to releasing a second volume of its Historical Record Report, to include AARO's findings on reports of potential pranks and inauthentic materials," then expected in late 2025. No such volume was located in the sources available for this dossier, which means the sole official channel through which the finding was to be documented had not delivered as of the research cutoff.

So the honest state of the record is this. A major newspaper reported a specific, named, dated practice sourced to a Pentagon office's internal review. The Pentagon has neither confirmed nor denied the practice, has said it cannot confirm the memorandum, has failed one FOIA search on appeal, has been ordered to search again, has promised a report volume that would address it, and has not published that volume. Every one of those is an artifact of an institution's conduct and every one of them is dated and attributable. None of them is the programme. YANKEE BLUE stays on this board as a node about the Department's disclosure behaviour, and it should not be cited by anyone as established fact about what was done to Air Force personnel until a document with a signature block exists.

In September 2025 OSD returned a categorical no-records FOIA determination, which its own appellate authority set aside on 12 December 2025 as resting on an inadequate search.

The Disclosure Project press conference at the National Press Club in Washington DC on 9 May 2001, at which about twenty of the project's sixty-nine interviewees spoke briefly and called for congressional hearings with subpoena power and amnesty for witnesses. ### Why it is implicated

Because almost everything attributed to it is unverifiable, and saying so precisely is worth a node. This press conference is the single most-cited event in modern disclosure advocacy, and the board can place exactly five people at its podium from opened sources. ### The longer account

The event is documented. Two sources of opposite disposition agreeing on date, venue and count is as solid as this subject gets.

In one line: Published 16 December 2017 by Helene Cooper, Ralph Blumenthal and Leslie Kean.

### What it is

'Glowing Auras and "Black Money": The Pentagon's Mysterious U.F.O. Program,' a New York Times article published 16 December 2017 by Helene Cooper, Ralph Blumenthal and Leslie Kean. It revealed the existence of the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program, its $22 million funding, its origin at the request of Senate Majority Leader Harry Reid, and its management by Luis Elizondo from the fifth floor of the Pentagon's C Ring. It was accompanied by the release of US Navy FLIR gun-camera footage.

### Why it is implicated

This is the date on which the concealment failed in public, and the article says so in its own lede: $22 million inside a $600 billion budget 'was almost impossible to find. Which was how the Pentagon wanted it.' The piece establishes three facts the program argument rests on: that the Defense Department had never before acknowledged the program's existence, that DoD said it shut down in 2012, and that the program's own backers said it did not, that officials continued to investigate episodes for five more years while carrying out other duties. That is a documented, contemporaneous dispute about whether a UAP program continued to run after the government said it had stopped. Every subsequent institutional response, the UAP Task Force, the 2021 ODNI assessment, AARO, the 2023 and 2024 hearings, the NARA records collection, is downstream of this article, and a whistleblower report given to Congress in 2024 alleges that a new Unacknowledged Special Access Program was created in 2017 in direct response to it.

### The longer account

The article is short on speculation and long on institutional fact, which is why it has held up. Its reporting basis is stated in the second paragraph: the program was documented 'according to Defense Department officials, interviews with program participants and records obtained by The New York Times.' Three sourcing streams, one of them documentary. Those records have never been published in full, and their contents remain one of the most valuable unexploited leads on the board.

The factual core is compact. The Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program was funded at $22 million within Defense Department budgets of roughly $600 billion a year. It was run by Luis Elizondo, a military intelligence official, from the fifth floor of the Pentagon's C Ring. The Defense Department had never previously acknowledged that it existed. DoD said it shut the program down in 2012. And, the sentence that does the most work, 'its backers say that, while the Pentagon ended funding for the effort at that time, the program remains in existence. For the past five years, they say, officials with the program have continued to investigate episodes brought to them by service members, while also carrying out their other Defense Department duties.'

That construction is the whole concealment argument in miniature. A program is funded through a line item that is hard to find. Its existence is not acknowledged. When it becomes inconvenient, funding is ended and the government says the program is over. The work continues anyway, distributed across officials' other duties, which is exactly the arrangement that makes a program invisible to the reporting requirements of 10 U.S.C. 119, there is no new SAP to notify, no budget line to report, and no milestone schedule to submit. Whether that is what happened is not proven by the article. What the article proves is that people with direct knowledge asserted it on the record in December 2017, and that DoD's contrary position was already a matter of dispute at the moment of first disclosure.

The money trail the article opened has been independently corroborated by the government's own subsequent releases. Steven Aftergood, writing for the Federation of American Scientists in January 2019 on the occasion of DIA's FOIA release of the 38 funded research titles, described the same program in the same terms: 'From 2007 to 2012, the DIA spent $22 million on the activity, formally known as the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program. It was apparently initiated at the behest of then-Senate Majority Leader Harry Reid, with most of the funding directed to a Nevada constituent of his', and cited this article by name. The Debrief's 2021 investigation into BAASS likewise anchors its $22 million figure to this piece. So the article's central financial claim has been ratified by a FOIA release, by an arms-control watchdog, and by a subsequent investigative outlet using different documents.

The article's second, less-discussed effect was on the evidentiary record itself. It carried Navy infrared gun-camera footage into mainstream circulation, which the Navy later confirmed as authentic imagery of unidentified aerial phenomena. That transformed the subject from a testimony problem into a sensor-data problem, and it is the reason later official documents, including the 2021 ODNI assessment's observation that a majority of UAP 'registered across multiple sensors, to include radar, infrared, electro-optical, weapon seekers, and visual observation', are framed in terms of instrumentation rather than eyewitnesses.

The third effect is the one that matters most for this board, and it is alleged rather than established. In written testimony to House Oversight on 13 November 2024, Michael Shellenberger reported that a whistleblower's report provided to congressional committees alleges that DoD created an Unacknowledged Special Access Program called IMMACULATE CONSTELLATION in 2017, after this article was published, to serve as a 'central or parent USAP' consolidating UAP observations and enforcing internal information security by detecting, quarantining and transferring collection incidents before they circulate within the Military Intelligence Enterprise. If true, this article did not merely expose a program, it caused a new and better-hidden one. DoD spokesperson Sue Gough has denied that any SAP by that name exists in its records.

There are also unresolved discrepancies that a careful analyst should hold onto. The article and FAS both date the program 2007-2012 and call it AATIP; the DIA contracting record dates the BAASS contract to 2008 and calls the effort AAWSAP; Shellenberger's testimony dates AAWSAP 2009-2017 with reports ending in 2010 and the program running through 2012. The government has never issued a clean, consistent account of the relationship between AATIP and AAWSAP, and that inconsistency is not incidental, it is the kind of ambiguity that survives only when nobody is compelled to resolve it.

What the article does not do: it does not allege retrievals, materials, or reverse-engineering. It does not name a contractor in its lede. Its claims about the program's survival past 2012 are attributed to unnamed backers, and its documentary basis has never been made public.

### What it has denied

The article itself records the Defense Department's position that the program was shut down in 2012, and simultaneously records that program backers say it continued, making the denial and its contradiction contemporaneous and jointly attributable. DoD's later flat denial of IMMACULATE CONSTELLATION, the program allegedly created in response to this article, is the second denial in the same chain.

Roles the board assigns it: disclosure, concealment.
